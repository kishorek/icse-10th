This chapter introduces organic chemistry through five questions:

1. What are organic compounds?
2. Why is carbon the central element of organic chemistry?
3. What is the basic difference between organic and inorganic compounds?

## 5. Lesson 1 — What are organic compounds?

### 5.1 Opening observation

A candle burns away. A steel nail held near the same flame does not burn away in the same way. Candle wax is mainly a mixture of hydrocarbons. The nail is mostly iron, a metal. The difference is a first clue to why carbon compounds are studied as a family.

### 5.2 Definition

> **Organic chemistry** is the branch of chemistry that studies carbon compounds, especially those containing carbon–hydrogen bonds, and their reactions.

### 5.3 Why “organic”?

Early chemists believed that a special “vital force” present only in living organisms was needed to make organic compounds. This was the **vital-force theory**.

In 1828, **Friedrich Wöhler** synthesized **urea** from **ammonium cyanate**, an inorganic compound, in the laboratory. This showed that an organic compound could be prepared without a living organism. The vital-force theory was abandoned. The name “organic” survived, but the modern definition is based on carbon-based structure, not origin.

### 5.4 What counts as organic?

In school chemistry, organic compounds are carbon compounds, usually containing hydrogen and often oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus or a halogen.

**Exam-ready definition:**

> **Organic compounds** are carbon compounds, usually containing hydrogen, excluding simple carbon compounds such as carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, carbonates, bicarbonates, cyanides and carbides.

Standard carbon-containing exceptions:

| Compound | Formula | Why it is treated as inorganic |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon monoxide | CO | Oxide of carbon |
| Carbon dioxide | CO₂ | Oxide of carbon |
| Calcium carbonate | CaCO₃ | Carbonate |
| Sodium bicarbonate | NaHCO₃ | Bicarbonate |
| Sodium cyanide | NaCN | Cyanide |
| Calcium carbide | CaC₂ | Carbide |

### 5.5 Worked classification

| Substance | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose | Organic | Carbon compound with C–H and C–O bonds |
| Methane | Organic | Hydrocarbon: carbon bonded only to hydrogen |
| Carbon dioxide | Inorganic | Oxide of carbon; standard exception |
| Sodium chloride | Inorganic | Ionic compound; contains no carbon |
| Ethanol | Organic | Alcohol with C–H and C–O bonds |
| Calcium carbonate | Inorganic | Carbonate; standard exception |

### 5.6 Common misconceptions

- “Every compound containing carbon is organic.” False: CO₂, carbonates, bicarbonates, cyanides and carbides are standard exceptions.
- “Organic compounds come only from living things.” False: many are synthesized in laboratories and factories.
- “Organic chemistry is separate because organic compounds are alive.” False: the separation is based on the number and variety of carbon compounds.

### 5.7 Quick-check answers

1. Organic compounds are carbon compounds, usually containing hydrogen, excluding simple carbon compounds such as CO₂ and carbonates. **Answer: the modern definition, not “all carbon compounds.”**
2. Wöhler synthesized urea from an inorganic compound. **Answer: true.**
3. Carbon dioxide contains carbon but is conventionally inorganic. **Answer: CO₂.**

## 6. Lesson 2 — Why is carbon the central element?

### 6.1 Carbon atom

- Atomic number: 6.
- Electronic configuration: 2,4.
- Valence electrons: 4.
- Carbon needs four more electrons to complete its octet.

### 6.2 Tetravalency

> **Tetravalency** is the property of carbon by which it forms four covalent bonds.

Carbon shares four pairs of electrons rather than losing or gaining four electrons. The four bonds can be with hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, a halogen, or other carbon atoms.

### 6.3 Catenation

> **Catenation** is the self-linking of carbon atoms to form straight chains, branched chains or closed rings.

Carbon–carbon bonds are strong, so the chains can be long and stable. A carbon skeleton can be:

- **Straight:** carbon atoms form a continuous line.
- **Branched:** a side chain is attached to the main chain.
- **Cyclic:** carbon atoms form a closed ring.

### 6.4 Single, double and triple bonds

| Compound | Formula | Carbon–carbon bond | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | Single (C–C) | Saturated |
| Ethene | C₂H₄ | Double (C=C) | Unsaturated |
| Ethyne | C₂H₂ | Triple (C≡C) | Unsaturated |

A single bond has one shared pair. A double bond has two shared pairs. A triple bond has three shared pairs.

### 6.5 Benzene preview

Benzene has six carbon atoms in a ring, with one hydrogen atom attached to each carbon. Its formula is **C₆H₆**. At this level it is drawn as a regular hexagon. A common simplified representation shows alternating double bonds; the app uses a dashed inner ring to suggest that the electrons are spread around the ring rather than fixed in one position. The full explanation belongs to a later stage.

### 6.6 Isomerism preview

**Isomers** have the same molecular formula but different structural formulae. For example, butane and 2-methylpropane both have the formula C₄H₁₀ but different carbon skeletons. This is one reason organic compounds are so numerous.

### 6.7 Enrichment — why not silicon?

Silicon is also tetravalent, but silicon–silicon bonds are weaker and silicon does not catenate as readily as carbon. Carbon's small size allows strong, stable bonds and enormous structural variety. This comparison is enrichment, not a requirement for this chapter.

### 6.8 Quick-check answers

1. Carbon has four valence electrons, so it forms four covalent bonds. **Answer: four.**
2. Catenation is the self-linking of carbon atoms. **Answer: catenation.**
3. A double bond has two shared pairs. **Answer: two.**

## 7. Lesson 3 — Organic vs inorganic compounds

### 7.1 Comparison table

| Property | Organic compounds | Inorganic compounds |
|---|---|---|
| Bonding | Mostly covalent | Often ionic; some covalent |
| Melting/boiling points | Generally low for simple covalent molecules | Generally high for ionic and giant covalent structures |
| Solubility | Usually insoluble in water; often soluble in organic solvents | Often soluble in water |
| Electrical conductivity | Generally poor; most are non-electrolytes | Ionic compounds conduct when molten or in aqueous solution |
| Combustion | Many burn in oxygen to form CO₂ and water | Generally non-flammable |
| Reaction rate | Often slower | Often faster, especially in ionic solutions |

These are general trends with exceptions. Some organic compounds are water-soluble; some inorganic compounds are covalent; not every organic compound burns.

### 7.2 Why the differences appear

Simple organic compounds are usually separate covalent molecules. The forces between molecules are weak, so less energy is needed to melt or boil the substance. There are no free ions, so the liquid or solution does not conduct.

Many inorganic compounds are ionic. Oppositely charged ions are held in a giant lattice by strong electrostatic forces, so melting points are high. When melted or dissolved, the ions become free to move and the substance conducts.

### 7.3 Like dissolves like

> Polar solvents such as water tend to dissolve polar or ionic substances. Non-polar organic solvents such as petrol tend to dissolve non-polar organic substances.

This explains why many organic compounds do not dissolve in water but do dissolve in petrol or ether.

### 7.4 Sorter answers

| Substance | Bin | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose | Organic | Carbohydrate with C–H and C–O bonds |
| Methane | Organic | Simplest hydrocarbon |
| Ethanol | Organic | Alcohol used in fuels and sanitizers |
| Urea | Organic | Nitrogen fertiliser; contains carbon |
| Polythene | Organic | Polymer made from ethene |
| Sodium chloride | Inorganic | Ionic; no carbon |
| Copper(II) sulphate | Inorganic | Ionic salt; no carbon |
| Carbon dioxide | Inorganic | Oxide of carbon; standard exception |
| Calcium carbonate | Inorganic | Carbonate; standard exception |
| Sodium cyanide | Inorganic | Cyanide; standard exception |

### 7.5 Common misconceptions

- “All organic compounds are flammable.” Too strong; many burn, but not all.
- “All inorganic compounds conduct electricity.” Only ionic inorganic compounds conduct, and only when molten or dissolved.
- “Like dissolves like means water dissolves everything.” No: polar solvents dissolve polar substances; non-polar solvents dissolve non-polar substances.

### 7.6 Quick-check answers

1. Simple organic compounds: low melting point, covalent bonding, poor conductivity.
2. CO₂ is a standard inorganic exception.
3. A non-polar organic compound is most likely to dissolve in petrol.

## 9. Lesson 5 — Why do organic compounds matter?

### 9.1 Importance

- **Life:** proteins, carbohydrates, lipids and nucleic acids such as DNA are organic.
- **Food and agriculture:** food is largely organic; urea and many pesticides are organic.
- **Health:** medicines, vitamins, antiseptics and medical materials.
- **Materials and clothing:** plastics, synthetic fibres, rubber, paints, adhesives and dyes.
- **Energy and transport:** petrol, diesel, LPG and natural gas.
- **Economy and work:** petrochemical, pharmaceutical, food, textile and agricultural industries.

### 9.2 Environmental costs

- Many plastics are not biodegradable and persist in the environment.
- Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas linked to climate change.
- Some organic solvents and pesticides are toxic or pollute water and soil.

“Organic” does not automatically mean “safe” or “environmentally friendly.” Natural organic compounds can be poisonous, and synthetic organic products can be harmful.

### 9.3 Quick-check answers

1. Urea is the organic nitrogen fertiliser.
2. Plastic pollution from non-biodegradable polymers is an environmental problem.
3. Organic chemistry supports medicines, fuels, food and materials.

## 10. Memory workshop

### Acrostic

**“Tiny Carbon Makes Isomers.”**

| Word | Target item | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Tiny | Tetravalency | Carbon forms four covalent bonds. |
| Carbon | Catenation | Carbon atoms link to form chains and rings. |
| Makes | Multiple bonds | Carbon can form single, double and triple bonds. |
| Isomers | Isomerism | The same molecular formula can give different structural formulae. |

**Recall prompt:** cover the table, say the sentence aloud, then rebuild the four items in order and explain each one.

The sentence is a real sentence, not an acronym. The order is meaningful: bonding capacity, self-linking, multiple bonding, structural variety.

### Additional memory aid

Picture carbon as a four-handed connector that can hold hands with itself. The four hands are its four covalent bonds; the self-linking is catenation.

## 11. Worked exam-style examples

### Example 1 — Classify and justify (4 marks)

Classify each substance as organic or inorganic, giving a reason: glucose, carbon dioxide, sodium chloride, ethanol.

**Answer:**

- Glucose: organic — a carbon compound with C–H and C–O bonds.
- Carbon dioxide: inorganic — an oxide of carbon, a standard exception.
- Sodium chloride: inorganic — an ionic compound containing no carbon.
- Ethanol: organic — an alcohol containing carbon–hydrogen and carbon–oxygen bonds.

**Marking points:** correct classification (2), correct reason for each (2).

### Example 2 — Explain carbon's versatility (4 marks)

Explain why carbon forms a very large number of compounds.

**Answer:**

Carbon is tetravalent: it forms four covalent bonds. It can catenate, linking to other carbon atoms to form straight chains, branched chains and rings. It can form single, double and triple bonds. Different arrangements of the same molecular formula produce isomers. These properties together allow an enormous number and variety of stable carbon compounds.

**Marking points:** tetravalency, catenation, multiple bonds, isomerism, with a clear link to variety.

### Example 3 — Compare organic and inorganic compounds (3 marks)

Compare organic and inorganic compounds under bonding, melting/boiling points and electrical conductivity.

**Answer:**

Organic compounds are mostly covalent. Simple organic compounds have low melting and boiling points and are poor conductors. Inorganic compounds are often ionic; ionic inorganic compounds have high melting and boiling points and conduct when molten or in aqueous solution. These are general trends with exceptions.

**Marking points:** bonding contrast, melting/boiling contrast, conductivity contrast, with “generally” language.

### Example 4 — Explain an observation (2 marks)

A simple covalent organic compound does not dissolve in water but dissolves in petrol. Explain using “like dissolves like.”

**Answer:**

Water is polar; petrol is a non-polar organic solvent. The organic compound is non-polar. “Like dissolves like” means non-polar substances dissolve in non-polar solvents, so the compound dissolves in petrol rather than water.

**Marking points:** water polar, petrol non-polar, compound non-polar, correct application.

## 12. Revision notes

### Key definitions

- **Organic chemistry:** study of carbon compounds, especially those with C–H bonds, and their reactions.
- **Organic compound:** carbon compound, usually containing hydrogen, excluding CO, CO₂, carbonates, bicarbonates, cyanides and carbides.
- **Inorganic compound:** compound not classified as organic; often ionic; may or may not contain carbon.
- **Tetravalency:** carbon forms four covalent bonds.
- **Catenation:** self-linking of carbon atoms into chains or rings.
- **Hydrocarbon:** organic compound containing only carbon and hydrogen.
- **Isomer:** same molecular formula, different structural formula.

### Structures at a glance

| Name | Formula | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| Methane | CH₄ | Four C–H single bonds |
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | One C–C single bond |
| Ethene | C₂H₄ | One C=C double bond |
| Ethyne | C₂H₂ | One C≡C triple bond |
| Benzene | C₆H₆ | Six-carbon ring |
| Ethanol | C₂H₅OH | Contains an –OH group |
| Urea | CO(NH₂)₂ | Contains carbon and nitrogen |

### Common mistakes

1. Treating every carbon-containing compound as organic.
2. Saying organic compounds come only from living things.
3. Confusing tetravalency with catenation.
4. Assuming all organic compounds dissolve in water.
5. Assuming all organic compounds burn.
6. Confusing a double bond with two separate bonds to different atoms.
7. Giving C₆H₁₂ for benzene; benzene is C₆H₆.

### Rapid revision sheet

- Organic chemistry is the study of carbon compounds and their reactions.
- Wöhler synthesized urea in 1828, challenging the vital-force theory.
- Carbon has electronic configuration 2,4 and forms four covalent bonds.
- Catenation is carbon's self-linking into chains and rings.
- Carbon forms single, double and triple bonds.
- Isomerism produces different structures from the same molecular formula.
- Organic compounds are generally covalent, low-melting and non-conducting.
- CO₂, carbonates, bicarbonates, cyanides and carbides are standard inorganic exceptions.
- Organic compounds are important in fuels, food, medicine, plastics and agriculture.
- Plastic pollution and fuel emissions are environmental costs.
